# Mechanisms & challenge notes

The parent artifact carries each direction's claim, insight, worked example, and verdict. This file carries what was too long to sit inline: the **full mechanism** — the decision procedure a reviewer would actually run — and the **full challenge**.

Verdicts: ✅ survives · ⚠️ weakened · ❌ does not survive. *Weakened* means nearly the same thing every time it appears: a sound, sourced insight carrying unvalidated procedural machinery. Separate the two before adopting either.

## How hard you may cut, and what a cut is allowed to touch

### Two compression budgets, tagged at the clause

**⚠️ weakened** — Decide how hard you may cut with one question — could a reader act correctly knowing only WHAT and in what ORDER, or do they need WHY — but tag the answer per clause, not per passage, and run a pinning pass before any cutting starts.

**Mechanism.** Three steps, in order. STEP 1, TAG PER CLAUSE. For every finite clause (own subject+verb; split at coordinating/subordinating conjunctions, semicolons, em-dashes) ask: does removing it change the reader's next physical action (PROCEDURAL) or their belief/risk assessment (EXPLANATORY)? A dependent clause inherits its host's tag UNLESS it contains a conditional or consequence marker attached to a safety/data/cost outcome (unless, only if, before/after + a stated consequence) — that marker forces EXPLANATORY regardless of the host. Clause grain, not passage grain, because mixed sentences are the norm, and passage-level tagging forces one verdict on a sentence carrying both. STEP 2, PIN BEFORE CUTTING. Scan the passage for clauses carrying a truth-changing modifier, a safety/data-loss/cost consequence, or a live inbound pointer. PIN them: wording may be tightened, the fact may not be removed, and the clause stays in the same visual unit as the step or claim it modifies — never demoted to a footnote or a later bullet. Where a pinned clause is a step's enabling condition, write it in a fixed slot rather than loose prose: '<N>. <imperative>. Requires: <prior state, named by step number or artifact>. If skipped: <the specific named failure>.' Write that slot ONLY when you can name the specific failure — if the honest answer is 'may cause problems' you do not know the dependency, so either find out or leave the step undecorated; a fabricated Requires clause is worse than none. STEP 3, BUDGET THE REMAINDER. Unpinned PROCEDURAL clauses: cut toward bare imperatives, validated by having someone actually run the steps and restoring detail only where they stall. EXPLANATORY clauses: cut filler only; every distinct trade-off, scope condition, sample size and limitation survives, even if that section ends up the longest in the document. TWO OUTER BOUNDS regardless of tag: past roughly 90% word reduction you are in the zone where over 70% of extreme abstractive summaries contained hallucinated content (Maynez et al. 2020); and no readability-formula score is ever the target. Reviewer test: point at any cut clause and ask what tag it got and what marker in the text justifies that tag.

**Fails when.** Clause boundaries are genuinely fuzzy under multiple subordination, and two reviewers will disagree about where one ends. The gaming surface moves rather than disappearing: an author under deadline can tag everything EXPLANATORY to protect it, exactly as they could previously classify a whole passage as explanatory to dodge the procedural budget. Pinning over-applied becomes a rationalization for protecting any clause the author is anxious about, so it must be scoped to a real truth-changing modifier or a real safety/data/cost consequence, not generalized nervousness.

**Steelman.** This is round 1's WHY-test direction with its own conceded flaw repaired: round 1 admitted it "presupposes a split-by-mode pass it did not supply" and contradicted the deletability direction on a quick-start-with-safety-precondition case. Moving the tag to clause grain removes the false premise that passages arrive pre-sorted into clean procedural or explanatory buckets, and sequencing pin-before-cut turns an unadjudicated tie between two directions into an ordering rule.

**Strongest counter.** The direction's own failure-mode section is effectively a pre-written rebuttal: clause boundaries are "genuinely fuzzy," two reviewers will disagree, and "an author under deadline can tag everything EXPLANATORY to protect it" — i.e., the gaming surface the mechanism was built to close simply relocates to a finer grain. Stripped of the one grounded citation (which supports only "content class affects compression payoff," a claim about corpora, not about clause-tagging procedure), the entire enforceable machinery — clause-grain classification, pin-then-budget precedence, the fixed Requires/If-skipped template — is labeled by the direction itself as "design inferences...

**Would be disproven if.** Give the same passage to two independent reviewers and have them apply the clause-tagging test (finite clause, PROCEDURAL vs EXPLANATORY, marker-forced override) independently. If they disagree on tags for a nontrivial share of clauses in ordinary technical prose (not the cherry-picked worked example), the "reviewer test" doesn't converge and the rule is unenforceable as stated.

**Verdict.** The grounded content-class insight survives; the clause-grain tagging apparatus and Requires/If-skipped template are unfalsified, self-admittedly untested inventions whose own failure-mode section documents the exact inconsistency and gaming risk that should disqualify them from an "enforceable by a reviewer" bar without a validation step this direction doesn't provide.

### What a cut is allowed to touch: operation class first, then dependency checks

**⚠️ weakened** — 'Cut ruthlessly' collapses three operations with three different risk profiles — removing a word, removing a claim, removing a qualifier that scopes a surviving claim — so name which one you are performing, then run the dependency checks at the intensity that operation warrants.

**Mechanism.** CLASSIFY THE OPERATION FIRST. WORD-LEVEL: function-word trims, intensifiers, filler, nominalizations reversed to verbs — the categories Williams/Lanham/Zinsser explicitly name. Risk near zero when scoped to those categories; license default-on, no review beyond a read-aloud check for garden-path misparses. CLAIM-LEVEL: removing an entire assertion — a sentence, bullet or clause stating a fact, result or recommendation. Risk high and binary; requires the full check set. QUALIFIER-LEVEL: removing or shortening a hedge, scope condition, sample size, confidence marker or exception attached to a claim that itself survives. The most dangerous and least visible of the three, because a diff of 'which claims are present' shows no change — the claim still stands, only its truth-value or applicability silently widened. Requires check (b) every time, with no word-count-driven exception. THEN CHECK, cheapest first. (a) GRAMMATICAL: function words are ~150 types but ~50% of running text and predict a large share of readability variance; they are not compression targets, and deleting 'that/which' invites garden-path misparses. Aim compression at nominalizations, expletive constructions, circumlocutions. (b) EPISTEMIC: does removing this modifier change the claim's truth-value or confidence? Scope conditions and calibration hedges stay, in the same visual unit as the claim — not a footnote, not a bullet three lines down. Filler hedges go, as do false hedges on directly measured results and methods. (c) INFORMATIONAL: no objective metric for this exists anywhere in the corpus and none should be invented, so force a written pre-commitment instead — before editing a list or a qualifier set for length, write in the PR or commit description 'if this item silently disappeared, what would change about the reader's next decision?' Written BEFORE cutting, so it cannot rationalize what was already removed. Apply extra scrutiny automatically when the answer names an item at the first or last position, since exceptions cluster at list edges and naive trimming starts at edges too. The point of writing it down is to convert an unreviewable mental judgment into a specific sentence a second reader can contest. (d) REFERENTIAL — the only check in this landscape a machine can run: extract every anchor link, footnote marker, and backward-pointing phrase ('as noted above,' 'for this reason,' 'see the X section'), plus demonstratives whose nearest plausible antecedent falls inside the deletion range; diff their resolved target line-ranges against the current hunk's deletion range and fail on intersection. Resolve by cutting the pointer with its referent, inlining a minimal restatement at the pointer site, or keeping both — never ship the fourth, unlisted outcome where the pointer survives and the referent doesn't. REVIEWER RULE that inverts the usual instinct: an edit changing word count while holding claim count and qualifier count constant needs no further review; an edit changing qualifier count while holding claim count constant needs the most scrutiny of the three.

**Fails when.** The three-way classification adds a step to every edit and gets skipped exactly where it matters most, on the qualifier cut that looks harmless; boundary cases exist where a long qualifying clause draws claim-level scrutiny purely by size. Checks (a) and (b) over-applied become 'better safe than sorry' and re-bloat the prose the guide exists to cut — they protect modifiers that change truth or confidence, not every modifier that adds precision. The (c) pre-commitment is clearable with a vacuous sentence ('nothing much would change') unless a second person reads and can contest it, which is a staffing cost the check does not itself supply.

**Steelman.** The taxonomy's real power is diagnostic, not procedural: naming qualifier-level deletion as a third, distinct operation — separate from word-level trims and claim-level deletion — explains why 'the summary was factually accurate but decontextualized' keeps recurring across separate facets of the research as if it were one diffuse phenomenon, when it is actually one specific, nameable failure mode that rides on the MD&A finding (context share 25%→09%, accuracy held constant).

**Strongest counter.** The word/claim/qualifier split is less clean in practice than the taxonomy assumes: removing a hedge-stack like 'in most configurations, particularly' is simultaneously a word-level trim and a qualifier-level operation, and real edits routinely straddle categories rather than sitting cleanly in one. Worse, the 'REVIEWER RULE that inverts the usual instinct' — no further review needed if qualifier count holds constant, maximum scrutiny if it changes — is circular: you cannot count 'qualifiers' without already having done check (b)'s epistemic work, so the shortcut smuggles back in the exact judgment call it claims to replace.

**Would be disproven if.** Have two independent editors classify a batch of real diffs into word/claim/qualifier-level using only the definitions given, with no discussion; if agreement falls much below roughly 70%, the taxonomy isn't doing the classification work it claims to do, and 'classify the operation first' collapses into 'argue about the classification' before any check can even begin.

**Verdict.** The qualifier-death insight, directly grounded in the MD&A finding, is the strongest and most original part of this direction and survives intact; the surrounding three-way classification apparatus and the 'machine-checkable' framing for check (d) claim more precision than the mechanism actually delivers, and the reviewer rule is partly circular.

### The hunk test for changelogs and PR descriptions

**⚠️ weakened** — Write a changelog or PR line the way a unified diff shows a change — the delta plus the minimum FROM-state needed to interpret it — never a bare assertion with zero anchor, never a restatement of everything that didn't change.

**Mechanism.** A unified diff never emits a hunk with zero context lines, because a change with no anchor is unlocatable to a reviewer who doesn't already hold the prior file state; it also never re-emits the whole file, because unchanged lines carry no signal. Apply the same test to each entry: does it state the delta AND the one clause of prior state needed to know what it changed from, AND one clause of consequence? An entry with only the delta — 'Added retry logic to the sync job' — is a zero-context hunk: retrying which failure mode, how many attempts, does it change behavior a caller depends on? It reads clean and is uninterpretable. Reviewer test: hand the line to someone who does not hold the prior behavior in their head and ask what changed and whether it affects them.

**Fails when.** Pulled too far, the context clause balloons into three sentences of background per line and the format degenerates into the full-file-replacement problem it exists to prevent. The clause must be the single fact required to interpret the delta, not a refresher on the feature. It also has no story for entries whose prior state is 'did not exist.'

**Steelman.** It converts 'write good changelog entries' from a vibe into a mechanical, teachable check by borrowing a rule engineers already internalize from diffs: a hunk with zero context lines is unlocatable, and a hunk that re-emits the whole file carries no signal. Applied to prose, that gives three concrete slots to check for (delta, minimum prior-state, consequence) instead of an unenforceable 'be clear and concise.' It is also scoped to exactly the genre the target-document list names and no other direction in the landscape reaches, so it isn't competing with a more general rule for the same territory.

**Strongest counter.** Its own grounding is an analogy, not evidence: the cited support is the MD&A decontextualization study (financial-summary compression, not changelogs) plus the diff-hunk metaphor itself, which is engineering intuition, not a reading-comprehension finding. No source in the provided research packet measures changelog or PR-description comprehension at all. Worse, the mechanism has a hole its own failure-mode only half-admits: a large share of real changelog entries describe wholly new capability with no meaningful FROM-state ('Added dark mode') — for those, the diff-hunk analogy simply doesn't apply, since a diff hunk is only coherent when there is a prior file to diff against. That's not an edge case to caveat, it's plausibly close to half of typical entries, which undercuts the mechanism's claim to generality.

**Would be disproven if.** Sample real CHANGELOG.md files from a set of popular OSS projects and classify entries as 'modifies existing behavior' vs 'pure addition with no prior state.' If pure-addition entries are a large fraction (say >40%), the hunk framing fails as a general mechanism and needs to be rescoped to modification-only entries. Separately, a reader test — hand engineers unfamiliar with a codebase changelog lines written with vs. without the prior-state clause and ask them to judge 'does this affect me' — that shows no accuracy difference would undercut the claimed payoff directly.

**Verdict.** The underlying worry (bare-delta entries are uninterpretable) is sound and consistent with the decontextualization research direction-wise, but the specific hunk-test mechanism has no dedicated evidence of its own, overclaims applicability to net-new-feature entries, and loses its head-to-head tension with the better-evidenced redundancy-debt direction on genre classification.

## Devices that carry — or destroy — logical structure

### The pairwise relation test: chained-in-time vs. crossed-in-state

**⚠️ weakened** — Test relation per PAIR of items, not per passage — a pair whose order can't be reversed without changing what's true is chained and belongs in prose with an explicit connective; a pair whose values combine independently is crossed and belongs in a grid; a passage containing both gets two artifacts, not one compromise.

**Mechanism.** STEP 1, TEST EVERY ADJACENT PAIR — not one pair the author picks, which is the loophole that let any list be rationalized as parallel. For each pair ask: could a reader learn item B's value before item A's and reach an identical outcome? YES → CROSSED (order of discovery is irrelevant; only the combination of values matters). NO → CHAINED (B is only meaningful once A has resolved). 'Changes' means one of exactly two concrete things, not an impression: TRUTH — the swapped statement, read literally, asserts something the source text does not support or contradicts; or ACTIONABILITY — a reader executing the items in swapped order would take an action they could not yet correctly take, or a wrong one. STEP 2, TIE-BREAK WITHOUT A THIRD VOTE. When two readers disagree, ask: does removing item N change what item N+1 means, read in isolation? If yes, the dependency is definitional — chained. If N+1 is fully self-contained without it, the pair is crossed regardless of how it feels. BURDEN OF PROOF: when no swap produces a concrete truth or action failure, the default is CROSSED — keep the bullets. This is deliberately the opposite of an author's instinct to protect prose-y explanation, and it exists because the single-pair version of this test was over-applicable to the point of banning nearly every list. STEP 3, ROUTE, AND ACCEPT TWO ARTIFACTS. Chained pairs → prose with an explicit connective (because / once / unless), using because-class wording rather than 'and' or 'after,' since only causal connectives have been shown to trigger causal inference; if bullets are kept anyway for scanning, the connective goes inside each bullet's own text, because visual adjacency is not a demonstrated cue for logical relation. Crossed pairs → a grid, scoped explicitly to the point in the sequence where they apply ('at steady state, after the canary window'), never an unscoped global table. A passage containing both kinds of pair yields a prose time-axis PLUS a scoped state-axis grid. Insisting on one glyph for the whole passage is optimizing the wrong thing. STEP 4, LABEL THE RELATION IN WORDS. A bare bullet list is not neutral — readers treat an undecorated list as an implicit AND, a set of independent facts that all apply, which is why an alternatives-list left undecorated gets misread as a checklist of requirements. Pick one of three fixed lead-ins and reuse the phrase literally: 'Do these in order:' / 'Choose one:' (or 'Any of the following:') / 'All of the following must hold:'. Test: read the lead-in as grammatically continuing into each item ('Choose one: JSON'); if that doesn't parse as the intended relation for every item, the wrong lead-in — or the wrong device — was picked. Never bold the lead-in, or it spends the section's emphasis budget on a label. Enforcement: a second reader runs the test independently; an author's classification of their own list doesn't count.

**Fails when.** All-pairs testing is O(n) by hand on a long list and O(n²) across conditions, and gets skipped under deadline; the practical fallback — test only the pairs a first read flags as suspicious — reintroduces the author-picks-the-pair loophole at smaller scale. The shortcut that keeps most of the value is to split on an obvious global phase marker ('once X / during X') first, then check pairwise within each phase. The tie-break assumes two independent readers exist; a solo author applying it to their own draft remains the weakest link.

**Steelman.** This is the only direction in the landscape that actually resolves, rather than merely flags, the loudest internal contradiction: 'the reorder-swap test' says chained content becomes prose, 'the table as completeness proof' says multi-condition content becomes a grid, and both fire on the same real passages because they were built to operate at passage granularity. Moving the unit of analysis to the pair is the correct fix; it's the same move formal logic makes when it stops asking 'is this argument valid' and starts checking each inference step.

**Strongest counter.** Every piece of cited evidence (Garner & Alley, Millis/Golding/Barker, Jansen, Tufte, McMaster) supports 'chained content loses something when bulleted' at the passage or connective level; none of it tests, or was designed to test, whether decomposing a passage into pairs and routing each pair separately produces better reader outcomes than picking one uniform treatment. The entire mechanism (pairwise unit of analysis, two-artifact output, three-phrase lead-in vocabulary) is explicitly labeled 'reasoned synthesis, not independently sourced' - honest, but it means the thing sold as the fix is untested at exactly the level it operates.

**Would be disproven if.** Construct or find a real passage containing a pair that passes both tests at once: swapping it changes a concrete truth-or-action outcome (chained) AND the pair's values combine independently of discovery order (crossed). If such a pair exists, the dichotomy doesn't partition cleanly. Separately: give the 'disputed pair' worked example, or an equally ambiguous real one, to five independent reviewers applying only the tie-break test; if they don't converge, the tie-break relocates disagreement rather than resolving it.

**Verdict.** Real conceptual advance over its predecessor (chained/crossed are properties of pairs, not passages), but the fix has zero empirical grounding at the level it operates, and its own stated complexity makes it viable only as a spot-check for disputed cases, not a rule run on every list.

### The table as a completeness proof, with escape hatches

**⚠️ weakened** — When a document states conditional behavior a table isn't a scannability choice, it's a verification device — and it only works if you band continuous conditions first, factor out mode switches before multiplying, and distinguish a cell that is impossible from a cell nobody has specified.

**Mechanism.** TRIGGER: two or more independent conditions determine an outcome (the crossed branch of the pairwise relation test). Build the grid — one column per condition, one row per combination — before writing prose about it. COMPLETENESS: multiply the cardinalities of the condition columns; the table must have that many rows, or an explicit documented collapse. CONSISTENCY: no two rows may give different outcomes for the same combination of condition values. Only after both checks pass may surrounding prose reference conclusions without re-enumerating them. CONTINUOUS CONDITIONS: 'latency exceeds a threshold' is not a set of discrete values and does not get gridded as one. Discretize into named bands with boundary ownership stated once ('<100ms' / '100-500ms, inclusive of 100' / '>500ms'); the grid applies to the bands, and inclusivity is not left to inference at each row. A condition that resists meaningful banding — a raw formula, not a decision point — does not qualify for this treatment at all and stays an inequality in prose; forcing it into a table manufactures false discreteness. CARDINALITY: before multiplying, check whether one condition GATES the relevance of the others. If maintenance=yes makes every other condition's value irrelevant to the outcome, it is a MODE SWITCH, not a grid dimension: split it out as a one-line phase rule ('during maintenance, all requests are rejected, full stop') plus a smaller grid over the remaining conditions. Repeat past roughly 4-5 remaining dimensions. A grid that still will not factor down is evidence the underlying rule set needs simplifying, not a formatting problem to solve with a bigger table. UNFILLABLE CELLS, two checks in order. (1) IMPOSSIBLE: can you cite a stated invariant elsewhere in the spec saying this combination cannot occur ('internal requests are always pre-authenticated by the gateway, per §3.2')? Mark the row IMPOSSIBLE with the pointer — never a blank cell, never a deleted row. (2) UNSPECIFIED: no citable invariant means the cell is not impossible, it is an open question in the spec; mark it UNSPECIFIED and route it to whoever owns the spec. When in doubt the default is UNSPECIFIED, not IMPOSSIBLE — claiming impossibility without a citable reason is exactly the undocumented gap the table exists to surface.

**Fails when.** Mode-gating factorization requires spotting a genuine gate versus a condition that merely correlates with the outcome; misidentifying one produces a phase split that hides real interactions the flat grid would have caught. The impossible/unspecified split is only as good as the invariant-citation discipline — a writer under deadline marks cells impossible on intuition rather than tracking down a citation, silently reintroducing the exact undocumented-gap failure the table exists to prevent. And nothing here says whether the resulting grid is readable on a narrow viewport.

**Steelman.** This correctly reframes the tired Google-vs-Microsoft one-row-table argument by changing the criterion entirely: a table earns its place by being falsifiable (multiply cardinalities, check the row count, check for contradicting rows), not by an arbitrary attribute-count threshold neither style guide can justify. That reframe is a genuine analytical move, not just a repackaging — it converts "does this look like a table" into "can this table be wrong," which a reviewer can actually check by counting.

**Strongest counter.** The grounded core (Parnas/SCR tabular expressions guarantee completeness and consistency) comes from formal requirements engineering for safety-critical software specifications — a genre and effort budget nothing like a README, ADR, or PR description. The direction's own "insight" concedes this stretch: banding continuous conditions, mode-gating factorization, and the impossible/unspecified split are labeled "reasoned extension, not independently sourced... should be read that way," meaning the entire practical apparatus for the target genres is invented, not evidenced.

**Would be disproven if.** Audit a sample of real READMEs, ADRs, guides, and PR descriptions for how often two or more independent orthogonal conditions actually determine a stated outcome (the trigger condition). If that pattern is rare in the target genres — as opposed to common in formal specs — this direction addresses a corner case dressed as a general prose-cutting principle.

**Verdict.** The reframe (falsifiability over attribute-count) is a real, citable contribution from Parnas; the banding/mode-gating/impossible-unspecified machinery built on top is unvalidated invented process for a genre where the triggering condition may rarely occur, and the direction's own failure-mode admits the extension can degrade correctness rather than guarantee it.

## Information order and budgeted repetition

### Fractal old-new, with a cold start and a licensed exception

**⚠️ weakened** — 'Lead with the important part' holds at three scales, not two — a document's or section's first sentence has no prior text to be 'old,' so it borrows its anchor from the title; sections open with the conclusion; sentences open with established information and land the new claim last, except where fronting new information deliberately corrects an assumption.

**Mechanism.** Four checks, two of them under a minute. COLD START. At position zero — a document's opening, or any section a reader might land on directly — there is no previous sentence to supply 'old,' and Gopen & Swan's topic/stress model has nothing to attach to. Borrow the anchor from outside the running prose: the subject of the first sentence must be the exact head noun of the title or heading (verbatim, never a bare category word like framework, tool, platform, solution), and the stress position must carry the one fact distinguishing this thing from its nearest alternatives, not a capability any competitor could also claim. Test, ten seconds: mentally swap a close competitor's name into the sentence. If it still reads true, the sentence said nothing about THIS thing. DOCUMENT/SECTION. Does the opening state the conclusion, so a reader who stops there still has the takeaway? SENTENCE. Does the subject noun phrase refer — verbatim, by pronoun, or by clear synonym — to something in the previous sentence, with the new claim landing last? A non-opening sentence whose subject is new information is a coherence violation even when it is grammatically active. TAIL-CHAIN TEST: underline only the last four to six words of every sentence in a paragraph and read the fragments alone, top to bottom. Pass: the fragments name a sequence of distinct new facts, each advancing the paragraph. Fail: generic filler ('as expected,' 'in this case,' 'for now') with no propositional content, or the same noun landing in the tail slot repeatedly, meaning the idea never advanced. SUBJECT-SWAP TEST: for every passive sentence, look at exactly one thing — its grammatical subject. Did this noun phrase (or its pronoun) appear in the immediately preceding sentence? Yes — the passive preserves a continuing topic, leave it. No — new information has been stuffed into topic position; rewrite active with the real agent as subject. One lookup per sentence, no judgment call about vagueness or agency. LICENSED EXCEPTION (marked theme). Halliday's framework has always contained the exception: new or contrastive information deliberately placed in topic position signals 'notice this, it overturns what you expected,' and works only because the surrounding prose follows the default. Two gates. FREQUENCY: at most one marked-theme sentence per section — used more often it stops reading as emphasis and starts reading as no consistent flow at all. CONTRAST: read the sentence immediately before the candidate. Does the fronted information contradict, narrow, or overturn something a reader would reasonably infer from it? Yes — earned. No — it is an ordinary violation, fix it the normal way. A reviewer applies this exception before flagging a sentence, not after.

**Fails when.** The cold-start swap test needs a real competitor to swap in; for a genuinely unique internal tool, fall back to the differentiation criterion — does the stress position add a fact absent from the heading itself rather than restating it. The chain test has nothing to anchor to in itemized reference content with no sentence-to-sentence flow by design, where forcing it manufactures connective tissue linking to nothing. A reviewer who doesn't hold the two-level distinction will 'fix' a correctly ordered passive into active because it superficially looks passive, breaking the very link it was preserving.

**Steelman.** This is targeted gap-closure at its best: round 1's 'Fractal old-new' explicitly admitted it had nothing to anchor to at a document's first sentence, and this direction supplies a concrete, testable answer — borrow the anchor from the title, and test genuine differentiation with a ten-second swap-in-a-competitor's-name heuristic a reviewer can actually run. The tail-chain test and subject-swap test go further, turning Gopen & Swan's well-evidenced topic/stress framework into something close to mechanical: not 'does this read well' but 'does this subject echo something in the prior sentence, yes or no' — a real reduction in required judgment, and one that correctly resolves the passive-voice fight as a level confusion (document architecture…

**Strongest counter.** Every mechanism specific to this direction's actual contribution — the cold-start swap test, the anchor-from-title move, and the licensed marked-theme exception with its frequency and contrast gates — rests on zero technical-writing-specific evidence, and the direction says so itself: 'No experimental evidence in this sweep isolates the marked-theme device in technical documentation specifically... a direct application of a cited linguistic framework, not a measured effect.' Gopen & Swan's own research examined sentence-to-sentence flow within continuous scientific prose, not the relationship between a title and an opening sentence; extending it to require a title-echo is a plausible but untested transfer, one step further from the data than even the NN/g first-two-words transfer this direction itself flags as borrowed by analogy.

**Would be disproven if.** Run a comprehension or takeaway-recall test where one arm opens documents with a title-noun-echoing, competitor-differentiating first sentence and the other opens with a generic capability statement; if readers extract the same core point at the same rate regardless of opening style, the swap-test recommendation loses the grounding it currently only asserts by analogy.

**Verdict.** The tail-chain and subject-swap tests are strong, well-evidenced operationalizations of Gopen & Swan and survive cleanly; the cold-start and marked-theme apparatus is honestly flagged as untested extrapolation and is considerably more elaborate than what is actually known to justify it.

### Redundancy debt, triggered by structural distance

**✅ survives** — Repeat yourself only where a reader could plausibly have lost the thread — measured by how many new concepts intervened and whether a heading came between — never on a fixed paragraph number, never in narrative sections, and most aggressively on the reference and how-to pages a search actually lands on.

**Mechanism.** TRIGGER 1, DISTANCE. For every clause whose sense depends on a concept established earlier, count two things: (a) how many new named entities or concepts have been introduced since that earlier mention, and (b) whether a section heading intervenes. Add a short backward-pointing clause naming the concept if EITHER (a) exceeds roughly four — Cowan's working-memory ceiling for material held without rehearsal or external support, which is exactly the condition unaided prose reading creates — OR (b) is true, because a heading is a plausible re-entry point and you cannot assume the reader read what sits above it. Test, 30 seconds: find the nearest antecedent, count intervening entities, and ask whether the sentence still parses for a reader starting at the nearest preceding heading rather than the top of the document. TRIGGER 2, GENRE. Tag sections expository (explains a system or argument) vs narrative (recounts a sequence — changelogs, postmortem timelines). Apply Trigger 1 in expository sections only: Delgado et al.'s meta-analysis found the screen comprehension deficit specific to expository text (g=-0.27) and statistically indistinguishable from zero for narrative (g=0.01). SCOPE THE HEADING-CROSSING HALF BY ARRIVAL MODE. Most visits to a doc-set start as browsing, not searching — a synthesis of nine usability studies across 1,500+ users found a mean 14% search-first rate (90% CI 11-21%, range 2-75% by site density) — so 'someone might land anywhere' is not the default case to design every section for, and hierarchical navigation, not search sophistication, is the primary findability lever. But the minority who do search don't land randomly: they land on reference and how-to pages, which exist to answer narrow googleable questions ('what does error X mean,' 'what's the default timeout'). Apply the heading-crossing trigger aggressively on reference and how-to sections, lightly on tutorial and explanation sections a narrow query rarely targets. On a reference section the satisfying form is one orienting line, not a re-explanation: name the parent system, version or mode, and convert vague backward references ('as described above') into a real link to the specific section. A heading no reader would search for ('## Details') fails on its own terms. WHAT WAS RETIRED, AND WHY. The 81/71/63/32 paragraph-position curve is gone as a trigger. It is aggregate fixation data pooled across mixed web content, not a measurement of paragraph-numbered technical documents, and 'paragraph 4' was never a claim the underlying study could support. What survives: Delgado's genre split (a direct meta-analytic finding about screen reading itself), Cowan's ~4-chunk ceiling under exactly the unaided condition linear reading creates, and the N400 finding that digital reading produces weaker semantic-network formation — none of which supports a general attention-decay-across-a-document claim.

**Fails when.** 'Entities since last mention' is not a parser output — a reviewer must judge what counts as a new named entity versus incidental vocabulary, reintroducing the judgment the paragraph-number version at least pretended to avoid, and four is a ceiling, not a target. This remains the easiest direction in the landscape to abuse into restating everything 'for skimmers,' re-bloating exactly what the guide exists to cut.

**Steelman.** This is the direction most willing to discipline itself: it explicitly retires its own earlier version's misused number (the 81/71/63/32 paragraph-position curve, aggregate fixation data never designed to support a literal 'paragraph 4' rule) and keeps only what the cited sources can actually carry — Cowan's ~4-chunk ceiling as a mechanism scoped correctly to the no-rehearsal, no-external-support condition that unaided linear reading creates, and Delgado et al.'s 54-study, 170,000-participant genre split (g=-0.27 expository vs g=0.01 narrative), which is a real, high-confidence finding in the research packet.

**Strongest counter.** The central enforcement number is a construct-validity leap dressed as precision: Cowan's ~4 chunks was measured on short-term recall of discrete stimuli under controlled lab conditions, not on how many named entities a reader can track across paragraphs of continuous technical prose before losing an antecedent. 'Count new named entities since last mention' treats discourse referent-tracking as if it draws on the same capacity, in the same units, as digit-span-style chunking — nothing in the provided research establishes that equivalence, and the reading-comprehension literature on anaphora resolution uses entirely different metrics (clause distance, competing referents) with different thresholds.

**Would be disproven if.** A reading-comprehension or eye-tracking study on technical prose (not digit-span tasks) showing readers reliably track referents across meaningfully more than ~4 intervening named concepts without misreading would undercut the specific numeric trigger. Separately, server-log or analytics data from an actual technical-docs site showing search-referral share for reference/how-to pages is not meaningfully higher than for tutorial pages would undercut the differential arrival-mode scoping that is this version's main addition over the retired one.

**Verdict.** Most defensible of the four: it polices its own prior overclaim, correctly separates two genuinely distinct redundancy principles by scope, and states its own evidence gaps rather than hiding them. The '4 entities or a heading' trigger should ship as a working heuristic to test, not as a citation-backed threshold — the number is borrowed from a different task paradigm and that gap is bigger than the direction concedes.

## Channels and budgets (carried forward from round 1)

### One emphasis budget per section, allocated by blast radius

**⚠️ weakened** — Treat every loud device as a withdrawal from one finite per-section budget, and allocate that budget by what a reader's misreading would cost — not by how important the author feels the claim is.

**Mechanism.** Two moves. (i) BUDGET: at most one loud device per section — bold span, callout, admonition — spent on the single highest-consequence claim. Bold works by contrast, so saturation destroys the signal it depends on; the phenomenon is well attested but the commonly quoted percentage threshold is unverified, so test rather than cite it. Test: strip every bold span, callout, and em-dash from a section and ask a cold reader which sentence mattered most — if the answer is 'can't tell' or 'all of them,' the formatting was decorative. (ii) ALLOCATION: for each unit, ask what happens if a reader misses or misreads it — cosmetic confusion, wasted time, or something unrecoverable (data loss, security exposure, safety, legal or contractual binding). High blast radius earns the section's one loud device and gets its consequence stated in the same visual unit, never in a separate warnings block. Restrict 'high' to irreversible, security/safety-relevant, or binding — not merely 'important.'

**Fails when.** Without a firm trigger, 'high blast radius' becomes a rationalization for verbose hedging on anything the author is anxious about. And a reviewer can over-read the budget half as 'formatting is always suspect,' discarding the sparse uses the evidence does support — this caps density and concentrates emphasis, it does not mandate zero formatting.

**Steelman.** This direction separates two axes the research kept conflating: content shape (causal vs. enumerable) and stakes (what a misread costs), and shows with a real number (the Google RCT's 3.5% legal vs. 14.6% PubMed gap) that stakes does independent work. Read that way, Tufte's Columbia slide and McMaster's 'not bullet-izable' aren't arguments against bullets-as-shape, they're arguments that failure-cost content needs different handling regardless of shape - meaning a guide organized only around causal-vs-parallel content lets a technically-parallel-but-lethal flag list through ungated.

**Strongest counter.** The direction refuses an unverified percentage threshold, then quietly substitutes an equally unverified count threshold: 'at most one loud device per section.' No source in the research measures how many bolded spans or callouts per section is optimal; the saturation effect is real directionally but unquantified at any granularity, count or percentage. The epistemic discipline is asymmetric: it polices the number's form without policing whether an equally-unsourced constant is better justified.

**Would be disproven if.** Find or construct a real section containing two genuinely independent irreversible/security/binding facts (not two aspects of one fact) where bolding only one, per the cap, left readers measurably worse-informed about the other relative to a version bolding both.

**Verdict.** The stakes-vs-shape axis separation is genuine and well-grounded in the RCT differential; the enforcement mechanism (a hard cap of exactly one) is exactly as unverified as the percentage threshold it pointedly avoids citing, just wearing a different unit.

### The serialization check

**✅ survives** — Every document is consumed twice — once as a two-dimensional visual surface, once as a linear stream where bold, position, whitespace, and layout do not exist — and only markup-level structure survives the second reading.

**Mechanism.** Run the draft through its linear channel and check it still works. (a) Read the heading outline alone: 71.6% of screen-reader users navigate long pages primarily by headings, making the outline a primary index rather than decoration — it must convey the document on its own, skip no levels, and every heading must answer 'what will I learn here' in a few concrete words ('Introduction / Overview / Details / More' serializes into a useless index). (b) Any meaning carried only by bold, color, position, or emoji must also exist in words — a screen reader announces 'face with steam from nose,' not your intent. (c) List capacity is set by which channel the reader is in: a visually re-consultable list (a persistent table, a sidebar, a numbered reference the reader looks back at) supports Miller-range lengths, while an unaided list — announced linearly, or one the reader must hold in mind while reading on — is Cowan-range; keep unaided lists short and split long ones with intervening prose, since a reader who hears 'List. 26 items' skips it outright. (d) Tables need th elements with scope and a caption, and any responsive reflow must restore header association or the grid collapses into a sequence of unlabeled values.

**Fails when.** The linear channel is a proxy, not a faithful model of every reader's experience, and over-applied the check argues against all visual formatting — which the evidence does not support. The rule is that visual emphasis must be redundant with structure, never that it's forbidden. It also says nothing about readers with cognitive disabilities beyond list length, or about non-native-English readers.

**Steelman.** This is the best-grounded direction of the four: 71.6% of screen-reader users navigating by headings, WCAG th/scope/caption requirements, and documented CSS-reflow semantic collapse (Roselli) are all high-confidence, directly-sourced findings, not extrapolations. Its genuine insight-level contribution is resolving the Miller-vs-Cowan fight by naming the actual moderating variable (external support/rehearsal, which the linear channel removes) rather than picking a side or splitting the difference arbitrarily — that is real analytical work, and it correctly reframes list-length as a consequence of a channel decision the author already made, not a fixed number to argue about.

**Strongest counter.** The unifying frame ("every document is consumed twice") quietly bundles three distinct populations and mechanisms — screen-reader users navigating by headings, sighted readers unable to visually re-consult a list, and general WCAG table semantics — as if they were one "linear channel" with one shared threshold, when the evidence for each is measured separately and for different reasons. More importantly, the insight overclaims: it asserts the accessibility findings are "the only available test of whether your scannability was structural or merely cosmetic," a normative ranking of channel-correctness as the definition of "real" scannability. Nothing in the cited evidence supports that priority claim — it's a values statement dressed as an empirical conclusion.

**Would be disproven if.** Run the same document through both a sighted visual-scan test and a screen-reader/TTS linearized read. If documents that pass the serialization check (clean heading outline, no meaning carried only by bold/color, short unaided lists) show no measurable improvement for the sighted-scanning majority over documents that fail it — i.e., visual scanners do equally well regardless — that would show the check is a necessary accessibility requirement running in parallel, not, as claimed, the definitive test of whether scannability is "structural or cosmetic" for readers generally.

**Verdict.** Grounding is genuinely solid and the Miller/Cowan resolution is a real contribution; the overclaim is narrow — dial back "the only available test" to "a necessary and previously-neglected test" and the direction holds without needing new evidence.

## When a figure or a code sample is the right device

### The multimedia split

**⚠️ weakened** — An independent research line (Mayer's multimedia principle) shows text+diagram beats text-alone regardless of bullet formatting, so 'add a diagram for structural content' stands on its own evidence rather than waiting on the bullets-vs-prose argument — but the same literature shows a figure that encodes no real structure is a seductive detail that reliably hurts.

**Mechanism.** Two tests before adding or keeping any figure. (1) STRUCTURAL TEST: does the content have a genuine spatial, sequential, or relational shape that prose has to serialize awkwardly — states and transitions, a call sequence across time, a topology of components and connections, a decision tree with more than about three branches? If yes, the multimedia principle predicts a real, independent gain: largest in Mayer's own within-lab studies (median ~89% gain, median d>1 across the 11 comparisons Clark & Mayer cite), re-estimated at a smaller but still real g=0.39 in a broader, more methodologically diverse 2020 meta-analysis. Treat the large number as lab-inflated and the medium one as the honest planning estimate. Pair every load-bearing figure with one sentence stating the assertion it proves, so the figure's conclusion survives into the linear channel. (2) SEDUCTIVE-DETAIL TEST: does the figure exist because the content structurally needs it, or because a wall of text 'needed a picture'? Material that encodes no structure but is merely interesting-adjacent — a decorative header graphic, a stock hero image, an icon standing in for a concept already stated in one clear sentence — runs against one of the most robustly replicated findings in this whole research pass: the seductive-details effect reliably reduces comprehension and recall by pulling attention from substantive content. The same over-inclusion logic applies inside a diagram: drawing every component obscures the essential structure.

**Fails when.** The structural test can be gamed — almost any content can be argued to have 'steps,' turning this into permission to add a diagram everywhere. It should fire only where prose already has to serialize genuinely parallel-in-time or spatially-related information (concurrent actors, branching state, component topology); a single linear list of sequential actions with no branching or concurrency does not need a diagram just because it is technically a sequence.

**Steelman.** The genuinely sharp move here is epistemological rather than empirical: instead of waiting for the still-nonexistent clean bullets-vs-prose RCT, this direction routes around Garner & Alley's confound entirely by pointing at an independent research program — Mayer's multimedia principle — that isolates diagram-addition while holding text format constant, meaning a style guide does not need the deconfounded version of the bullets debate to exist before it can recommend diagrams for structural content.

**Strongest counter.** None of the specific evidence this direction leans on — Clark & Mayer's '11 studies, median 89% gain, median d>1,' the 'Guo et al. 2020... g=0.39' re-estimate, the Garner/Brown/Sanders/Menke 1989 seductive-details foundation, or the signaling/segmenting effect-size table (d=0.52, d=0.35-0.38, g=0.24, g=0.32-0.36, g=0.19) — appears anywhere in the provided 8-facet research sweep or the depth-repair round I was given to check against. The idea landscape's own coverage_gaps section explicitly names this whole domain as untouched by the actual research process ('Diagrams, images, and code samples are entirely unaddressed... no direction says when a figure beats prose').

**Would be disproven if.** Pull the actual Clark & Mayer (2016) Cambridge Handbook chapter and the specific 'Guo et al. 2020' meta-analysis and confirm whether the '89% / d>1' and 'g=0.39' figures are accurately transcribed and attributed; if they can't be located or don't match, the numbers carry false precision even if the general effect they describe turns out to be real.

**Verdict.** The structural insight — separate multimedia-principle evidence from the confounded RCT, veto decorative figures via seductive-details — is sound and usable as a heuristic with no numbers attached at all, but every specific effect-size citation was never verified by this project's research process and should not ship as currently sourced.

### Code answers how; prose answers why, which, and together

**⚠️ weakened** — Robillard's field study of 83 Microsoft developers found code examples become 'more of a hindrance than a resource' precisely when readers need composition, rationale, or best-practice information a snippet was never built to carry — so a snippet is load-bearing for mechanical 'how do I call this' questions and actively frustrating as a substitute for prose.

**Mechanism.** Before defaulting to 'show code, skip prose,' classify the reader's blocking question using Robillard's own failure categories, in order. (a) MECHANICAL — do I have the right syntax, signature, basic call? A snippet answers this well; this is the case code was built for. (b) COMPOSITIONAL — how do multiple calls fit together across a scenario? This is where the data shows snippets fail hardest: individually correct calls with no connecting prose left developers unable to see the whole picture and hitting exceptions they had no way to anticipate. Add prose connecting the calls (or a diagram, per 'The multimedia split'), not another snippet. (c) RATIONALE — why this design, why this option among alternatives? Snippets cannot carry design intent; participants explicitly went looking for a person or a book when a snippet couldn't answer 'why is this like that.' Prose-only territory. (d) BEHAVIORAL-CONFIRMATION — does this match how the system actually behaves, or am I about to be surprised? Snippets carry a documented currency problem (aging examples that still run but reflect outdated practice); a bare snippet answers this worse than a snippet plus one sentence stating its version and scope. Trigger prose only where the specific failure is present — a snippet demonstrating one self-contained call needs no surrounding essay.

**Fails when.** The four-category test requires correctly guessing the reader's blocking question, which a writer may get wrong — over-applied, it becomes license to wrap every snippet in paragraphs of rationale 'just in case,' re-bloating exactly what code samples exist to avoid.

**Steelman.** If Robillard (2009) is accurately characterized, this is a real upgrade over 'show code, skip prose' as an unconditional rule: instead of a blanket instruction, it gives a four-way taxonomy of the specific questions a snippet cannot answer (composition across calls, design rationale, behavioral currency) versus the one it answers well (mechanical syntax), turning 'is this doc missing something' into a diagnosable, nameable failure category rather than an author's hunch. It also correctly reframes the trade-press folk claim ('developers just want code') by citing the reported reading-vs-example split running the other way, which — if accurate — flips the naive intuition the direction replaces.

**Strongest counter.** None of the direction's supporting citations — Robillard 2009, the 78%/55% reading-vs-example statistic, the C++ Stack-Overflow-to-GitHub vulnerability-propagation study — appear anywhere in research.json or depth_repair.json, the two files constituting the research base for this pass. This entire cluster ('when a figure or code sample is the right device') is also absent from ideas.json's five clusters; the landscape's own coverage_gaps section flags 'diagrams, images, and code samples are entirely unaddressed' as an open gap, meaning this direction was never run through the same contradiction-check and gate process the other four survived.

**Would be disproven if.** Locating and directly reading the actual Robillard 2009 text to confirm or correct the 78%/55% and 50-of-74 figures would resolve the immediate verifiability gap. A modern (post-2020) large-sample replication of the obstacle taxonomy — especially one accounting for LLM-assisted composition and inline-hover documentation, which didn't exist in 2009 — finding that 'compositional' and 'rationale' failures are now materially rarer would undercut using this specific taxonomy as a current-day baseline.

**Verdict.** The qualitative insight is plausible and non-cliché if the underlying paper checks out, but it is ungrounded in the provided research packet, was never cross-examined against the existing idea landscape's contradictions, rests on statistics I cannot verify, and has the weakest enforcement mechanism of the batch.

## Where content lives, and what moving it costs

### Route it, don't delete it

**⚠️ weakened** — Most verbosity is misplacement, so before cutting a passage decide its destination — main flow, marked footnote/appendix, or a different document entirely — and delete only what has no destination.

**Mechanism.** Two routing tests run on every passage you are tempted to cut. MATERIALITY: does omitting this change the reader's next action — their next command, click, or decision? Yes, it stays in the main sentence with its connective intact. True and relevant but doesn't change the next action: it moves to a marked footnote or appendix; it is not deleted and it does not stay inline bloating the flow. MODE: label each paragraph tutorial / how-to / reference / explanation. A paragraph in a different mode from the section around it isn't verbose, it's misfiled — relocate it to the document that owns that mode instead of compressing it in place. A section you cannot label with a single mode is itself the defect; split it before doing anything else.

**Fails when.** Materiality is reader-relative — a detail immaterial to a typical user is material to an SRE debugging at 3am — so 'not material' becomes a license to shunt anything inconvenient. And routing to an appendix nobody opens is deletion with extra steps plus a maintenance cost.

**Steelman.** This is the best-grounded of the four: Diataxis mode-separation and the MD&A decontextualization study (25% to 9% context share under compression) are both solid, on-point, high-confidence findings, and the central move - giving deletion a destination test instead of treating every cut as terminal - explains a real phenomenon: documents that get shorter without getting better. A materiality test tied to 'does this change the reader's next action' is sharper and more falsifiable than 'is this important,' and routing by Diataxis mode gives a second, orthogonal test for content that isn't verbose so much as filed wrong.

**Strongest counter.** The opening claim, 'most verbosity is misplacement,' is not supported by anything in the research and sits in tension with literature this same landscape draws on elsewhere: Zinsser's clutter-elimination, Williams' five concision principles, and Lanham's Paramedic Method are all about words that carry zero information and have no destination anywhere - 'currently' to 'now,' 'in order to' to 'to,' expletive constructions, redundant intensifiers. None of that is misfiled, it's dead weight, and the same research sweep says most first drafts can be cut roughly in half on exactly this basis. If 'most verbosity is misplacement' were true, mechanical word-level compression should recover little value, but it's independently well-evidenced as effective.

**Would be disproven if.** Take a set of real edited drafts (before/after) and classify each cut as either relocatable content that changed address, or content with no possible destination that was simply excised. If the large majority of effective cuts fall into the latter, as the Zinsser/Williams/Lanham material implies, 'most verbosity is misplacement' is empirically false even though the routing test remains valid for the minority it targets.

**Verdict.** The materiality/mode dual-test is sound and well-cited; the framing claim overstates its scope against the landscape's own equally-solid compression literature, and the failure-mode section half-concedes routing often degrades to deletion with paperwork.

### The context-fit tax: reuse is not free, it's a debt collected at every call site

**⚠️ weakened** — Before promoting a passage into a shared or transcluded unit, run a self-containment test — content that fails it doesn't save words on extraction, it moves the missing words to every call site instead.

**Mechanism.** SELF-CONTAINMENT TEST: strip the candidate passage of all deictic backward references ('this,' 'that,' 'as discussed,' 'for our case') and ask whether it reads correctly cold, to a reader who has never seen the surrounding document. PASS (a definition, a fixed procedure step, a constant, a value that means the same thing to every reader) → genuinely reusable; extract it, replace inline text with a reference or transclusion, and one edit now fixes every site. FAIL (its correct meaning depends on which audience or which surrounding argument is asking — the context-fit problem) → do not force a single shared unit. Either write two or more short call-site-local versions, or keep the passage duplicated inline and track the duplication explicitly — a comment naming which other sites must stay in sync and when it was last checked — rather than pretending one reused paragraph serves every reader equally well.

**Fails when.** The self-containment test is easiest to fake on procedural and reference content — numbers, fixed steps — which is also where reuse is legitimately cheap, so a writer under DRY pressure will apply it loosely to explanatory content, rationalizing away audience-dependence because extraction feels efficient. And 'duplication tracked explicitly' is a commitment with no tooling behind it in prose, unlike DITA's conref, which is machine-enforced; an untracked duplicate is just future drift with a clear conscience. Nothing in the corpus measures how fast duplicated technical prose actually diverges.

**Steelman.** This fills a gap round 1 explicitly flagged as uncovered ("cross-document architecture... the maintenance cost of redundancy... every direction here operates within a single document"), and it correctly identifies that DRY-for-prose differs from DRY-for-code because meaning, unlike executable behavior, is audience-relative — a paragraph can be simultaneously true and wrong-for-this-reader in a way a shared function can't be. The self-containment test (strip deictic references, ask if it reads correctly cold) is a crisp, reviewer-runnable heuristic, and the two-audience worked example (PM goals vs. developer edge-cases from one artifact) is a concrete, plausible illustration of a real failure people have actually hit.

**Strongest counter.** I checked the entire research sweep provided for this pass (research.json, depth_repair.json, ideas.json — 72 findings plus contradictions/unverified/depth sections) for every term this direction cites as its evidentiary base: "DITA," "conref," "Heretto," "CIDM," "Henderson," "ASIST," "context-fit," "single-sourc[ing]" — zero matches anywhere. This direction's entire grounding sits outside the verified corpus this challenge pass is supposed to check claims against; it may be accurate, but it hasn't cleared the same bar the other three directions' citations did.

**Would be disproven if.** Locate and verify the primary sources actually claimed (the DITA/conref authoring literature, the CIDM 2010 paper, Heretto's stated example, Henderson 2011 in ASIST) against their original text; if they say what's claimed, confidence should rise substantially and this concern dissolves. **Verdict.** The underlying intuition is sound and fills a genuine coverage gap, but the direction's citations are entirely unverifiable against the research corpus supplied for this pass (confirmed via exhaustive grep, zero hits), and its own best citation admits no data exists for the very failure mode (duplication drift) its prescription is meant to manage — this needs an explicit 'grounding unverified, confidence low' flag, not silent inclusion as sourced…

### The link is a leaky pipe: budget cross-references by actuarial class

**⚠️ weakened** — A cross-reference carrying load-bearing information is a bet that the target still exists and still means the same thing later — and that bet's size is measurable and differs enormously by reference class, so route load-bearing facts inline and reserve links for what age can't break.

**Mechanism.** Classify every outbound reference at write time. CLASS (a): same version-controlled doc-set, covered by an automated link checker in CI — near-zero URL death, but still exposed to silent REFERENT DEATH (the target still resolves but the section was edited to mean something else, since nothing in version control connects a code or doc change to the other documents referencing it). CLASS (b): external, third-party-controlled — another company's docs, a blog post, a forum answer — subject to measured decay, single-digit percent per year compounding, with permanent-loss rates of 40-70%+ observed over 9-15 year horizons. CLASS (c): archived or pinned snapshot — solves URL death but freezes content, unusable for anything expected to update. LOAD TEST: for any fact the reader needs to complete their task or trust a current claim, ask 'if this link died today, would the reader be stuck or wrong?' If yes and the target is class (b), inline the fact — state the load-bearing number or step directly and cite the link only as provenance, not as the delivery mechanism. If the target is class (a), the fact may live behind the link only if CI re-validates the referenced section's content on every change to either side; a checker that pings for HTTP 200 does not protect against referent death.

**Fails when.** Inlining every load-bearing fact defeats the real benefit single-sourcing provides for genuinely stable, centrally controlled facts, and reintroduces the duplication-divergence risk this landscape has no data to bound. The CI escape hatch requires content-assertion infrastructure most teams don't have — not just URL-status checkers — so it is easy to claim the safe class while actually operating in its unmonitored version.

**Steelman.** This correctly names an assumption 'link to the single source of truth' quietly makes: that a same-repo cross-reference and a link to a third party's changelog are equivalent-risk citations, when they manifestly are not. It responds with a cheap, actionable test — 'if this link died today, would the reader be stuck or wrong' — that a reviewer can apply to a single sentence without needing the underlying actuarial numbers to be exactly right, and it is the only direction in this landscape that reaches cross-document architecture at all, a domain the idea landscape's own coverage_gaps flags as entirely untouched.

**Strongest counter.** Like the multimedia direction, none of this direction's core citations — the Zittrain/Albert/Lessig Harvard Law Review Perma figures, the Aslib/Emerald 20-year LIS study, the CJR NYT deep-link crawl — appear anywhere in the provided research sweep; this is evidence introduced outside the project's verified pipeline for a domain explicitly flagged as an open gap. More substantively, the direction's own honesty undercuts its central framing: it admits 'no source measures internal, tooled doc-set link or referent decay, so applying these rates inside a repo is extrapolation' — yet the most consequential case for the target genres (README/PR/ADR cross-references are overwhelmingly Class A, same-repo) is exactly the case with zero measured decay rate, backed only by a single practitioner-blog quote standing in for data.

**Would be disproven if.** Audit same-repo cross-references in a real docs-as-code corpus (e.g., sampling 'see X section' pointers against the git history of both endpoints) to see how often referent-death actually occurs uncaught; if it's rare because ordinary PR review already catches most of it, the alarm this direction raises about internal links specifically is undercut, leaving only the external-link half (which at least has real, if genre-mismatched, backing).

**Verdict.** The external-link half — inline load-bearing facts, cite links only as provenance — rests on real, famous, independently-checkable studies even though absent from this project's own research file; the internal-link half of the actuarial taxonomy is asserted rather than measured and its proposed CI mitigation is conceded to be impractical for most teams.

## How any of this actually gets checked

### Enforcement-tiered rule taxonomy: machine, second reader, real user

**⚠️ weakened** — Organize the guide by how each rule gets checked rather than by topic — tag every rule machine-checkable, review-checkable, or only-testable-with-users, give each tier its actual enforcement plumbing, and demote any rule that fits no tier to 'principle' instead of publishing it as a rule.

**Mechanism.** Sort every rule into exactly one tier before it enters the guide, and publish the literal check beside it. TIER 1, CI, deterministic, no judgment: heading hierarchy and no skipped levels; broken links; th/scope/caption on tables; banned-word lists; sentence- and line-length thresholds; executable code examples; the referential-pointer diff. The enforcement line names the job that fails the build. TIER 2, review-checkable — requires judgment but reduces to a specific yes/no question a second reader can answer in under a minute: the pairwise relation test, the clause-level WHY tag, the hunk test, the cold-start swap test, the tail-chain read. Enforcement requires two things or it is not enforcement: (i) a CODEOWNERS-routed reviewer on the relevant paths, so the check is never skipped for lack of an assignee, and (ii) the rule phrased in the PR template as a closed question with a required answer ('Run the swap on every adjacent pair in any list you touched: does reversing a pair change what's true? Y/N — if Y, did you convert to prose with an explicit connective?'), never as open guidance like 'use lists well.' An author's classification of their own work does not satisfy a Tier 2 check. TIER 3, only testable by watching a real reader attempt the real task: whether a compression cost comprehension, whether a reordering helped, whether the reader left with the correct belief. Enforcement is a recurring sampled audit, not a per-PR gate. Each quarter take 3-5 high-traffic or high-stakes docs, including at least one procedural and one explanatory; recruit a handful of readers unfamiliar with them; give them the actual task or decision the doc supports (get the service running; state the one condition under which the Postgres decision would reverse); measure task success, error rate and time — never a formula score. Route every failure back to the specific rule that produced the failing passage, not just to the document: a rule that caused a task failure gets revised or gets a documented exception. A rule that cannot be assigned a tier is rewritten until it can, or moved to a principles preface that makes no enforcement claim.

**Fails when.** Teams write everything as Tier 1 because it feels rigorous, producing linter rules that cannot detect what they are named for (a regex cannot tell whether a bullet hides a causal relation), or everything as Tier 2 because it avoids infrastructure cost, which merely relabels 'ignored' as 'reviewable.' Either misclassification is worse than an honest Tier 3 label, because it manufactures confidence the rule is being checked. A PR template accumulating ten closed questions gets rubber-stamped — the shelfware pattern moved from the wiki into the template.

**Steelman.** This is the direction that actually answers the landscape's own most conspicuous unaddressed gap — coverage_gaps names 'validation and enforcement mechanics' as an open hole and specifically flags Martinez, Mollica & Gibson's 2024 finding (real, in research.json, high confidence) that 50 years of plain-language mandates produced no measurable decline in processing-difficulty features in US legislation.

**Strongest counter.** Two of its four supporting citations — the LintMe paper (arxiv 2603.00331) and 'Understanding Code Understandability Improvements in Code Reviews' (the 42% figure) — do not appear anywhere in the provided research packet and I cannot independently verify them. Bacchelli & Bird 2013 is a real, well-known paper I recognize from training, but it studies code review, not documentation review; the direction's own grounding section concedes this is 'extrapolated ... which no located study measures directly,' meaning the psychological justification for Tier 2 (reviewers won't systematically catch subtle judgment calls unless directed to a specific question) is analogy, not documentation-specific evidence. More seriously, the framework accurately diagnoses its own likely failure mode for Tier 2 ('everything as Tier 2 ...

**Verdict.** The core move — name the actual check beside every rule, and demote anything that can't be assigned one — is the right instinct and is well-grounded via Martinez/Mollica/Gibson, which is real, in the provided research, and exactly on point.

### Anchor perishable claims to something that fails the build

**✅ survives** — Co-locating docs with code fixes where documentation lives, not whether it stays true — so tag every factual claim stable or perishable, and write perishable ones so they either execute, get asserted against the source, or never state the volatile value at all.

**Mechanism.** Tag each factual claim at write time. STABLE — behavior, invariant, architectural decision, the why. Safe in prose; it changes rarely and deliberately, and a human is likely to revisit the doc when the decision itself is revisited. PERISHABLE — a specific default value, version number, file path, exact count, currently-true configuration, or a diagram of a topology that changes at code speed. For each, pick one of three: (a) GENERATE it at build time from the actual source (config schema, constants file, OpenAPI spec) so the doc cannot state a value the code doesn't have; (b) TEST it — a CI assertion failing the build when documented value and source value diverge, including doctest-style execution of the literal quickstart commands (`pytest --doctest-glob='README.md'`) and a link checker on every PR; (c) if neither is feasible, don't state the value — point to where it lives ('set by `DEFAULT_TIMEOUT` in `config.py`'), so the doc can only become less convenient, never wrong. DIAGRAMS follow the same rule: a diagram of something that changes at code speed goes in as text source (Mermaid, PlantUML, DOT, D2, Structurizr) rather than an exported image, so it produces a visible diff in the same PR as the change that invalidated it and a reviewer can ask 'should the diagram change too?' the way they'd ask about a missing test. Two litmus tests before treating any diagram as load-bearing: is every named component traceable to something that exists in the current code (a diagram failing this is worse than none, because it misinforms with no signal that it's stale), and — if the system cannot be expressed in a reasonably simple diagram, that is diagnostic of the design's complexity, not a communication problem to paper over with more boxes. TWO BOUNDARIES. Do not force a why-claim, hedge, or scope condition into an executable assertion — route it to review or user testing rather than distorting it to fit a linter. And if a readability-formula score is computed at all, log it as a trend line for a human to glance at; never gate a merge on it.

**Fails when.** Teams over-invest in the executable tier because a green pipeline feels like proof, then treat 'all doctests pass' as evidence the docs are good — executable checks only cover claims with a checkable output, and a document can pass every doctest while carrying a decontextualized, misleading rationale. Green CI is necessary, not sufficient, and the guide must say so next to the badge. Text-source diagrams remove the mechanical excuse for staleness, not the incentive problem: a team that ignores stale prose will ignore a diagram-source diff nobody is required to review.

**Steelman.** This is the only direction that answers a question none of the others ask: not how text should be shaped, but how a rule survives six months of nobody re-reading the style guide. It correctly identifies that the failure mode isn't staleness per se but staleness indistinguishable from currency - a reader has no signal to go verify a sentence that reads exactly as confident whether true or six months stale.

**Strongest counter.** The causal chain the mechanism depends on - things checked by CI stay accurate, things not checked silently rot - is asserted on industry blog posts (Sync-o, DataHub, Falconer, Ben Morris) the direction itself labels 'practitioner consensus only, no controlled study located,' and one supporting citation (Dasanayake et al. 2019) is explicitly excluded as unverifiable. That's honest sourcing, but the load-bearing claim of the whole direction - that executable enforcement actually outperforms unenforced convention at keeping docs true over time - has no measured support anywhere in this research sweep, only engineering intuition.

**Would be disproven if.** Find teams or repositories that adopted pytest-doctest-glob, link-checking, or diagrams-as-code and measure whether their documentation's actual error/staleness rate over 6-12 months is lower than comparable teams without these mechanisms - versus finding the enforcement mechanisms themselves get disabled or narrowed at a rate that erases the advantage.

**Verdict.** Best-scoped and most self-aware of the four, with explicit boundaries that keep it from overclaiming - but its central mechanism rests on anecdotal industry-blog causal reasoning never independently verified in this sweep, and it has no answer for enforcement-atrophy, the same decay problem it exists to solve.
