An alert goes through four steps before it reaches a person: it is matched to a rule, grouped with similar alerts, held for a short delay, and sent to the channel the rule names. Most routing surprises come from the delay and the grouping.

Describes the alert router as configured on 14 Oct 2026.

Matching an alert to a rule

Each alert arrives with a service name, a severity and a few labels. The router checks the rules from the top of the list and uses the first one that matches. Rule order therefore matters more than rule count: a broad rule near the top hides every narrower rule below it.

An alert that matches no rule goes to the catch-all channel, which is read once a day. If something important arrives late, check this first.

Grouping similar alerts

Alerts with the same service and the same rule are grouped into one notification. The group waits for 30 seconds after the first alert so that a burst becomes one page instead of forty.

What makes two alerts similar

Service and rule, and nothing else. Two alerts for the same service in different regions are still one group.

What happens as more arrive

The notification is updated in place with a count. It does not page again unless the severity rises.

The delay before sending

Warnings wait 5 minutes and critical alerts wait 1 minute. If the alert resolves during the wait, nothing is sent. This is why a brief spike never pages anyone, and also why a real outage can take a minute to reach you.

Sending to a channel

A channel is a place a person is reachable: the pager, the team chat, email. Each channel has an owner who decides who is on it, and the router never sends to a person directly.

If a channel has nobody on it, the router sends to its owner and writes a line in the audit log.

Escalation when nobody answers

An acknowledged page stops escalating. An unacknowledged one goes to the secondary on-call after 10 minutes, and to the engineering manager after 15. Acknowledging does not resolve the alert, and an acknowledged alert that stays open for an hour pages again.

Finding out why an alert went where it did

Open the alert and choose its routing trace. It lists the rule that matched, the group it joined, the delay it waited and the channel it was sent to, with a timestamp for each step. The trace is kept for 30 days, so a question about last month's page can still be answered.

If the trace shows a rule you did not expect, move that rule down the list rather than editing it, which keeps the change easy to undo.
